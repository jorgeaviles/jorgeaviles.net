## Define the question before the model

The historical Random Audit program supplied reviewed bills from which to construct labeled training examples. The implemented target is whether the review's reduction field is populated: a recorded zero counts as positive, while a null or blank field counts as negative within that reviewed sample. This predicts a recording convention associated with bill review, rather than the amount reduced or selection into the random program. It is not a label for fraud, proof of improper billing, or a direct instruction to initiate a full audit.

Bills outside the matched reviewed sample do not become negative examples simply because no review outcome is available. A blank reduction field also does not independently establish that a review was complete or that billing was proper. Label construction required attention to missing information, source recording rules, and matching outcomes to the correct bill. A model learns this defined target, including the limitations of its historical coverage and review practices. A useful ranking therefore needs a narrower interpretation than “which bills are wrong?”

## Engineer features with historical context

I developed features covering billed amounts and hours, service timing, submission lag, case context, and attorney billing history. Rolling 30-, 90-, and 365-day measurements describe prior attorney activity, while case-level cumulative features distinguish earlier work from the current bill. Assignment timing, certification, and court and case attributes add context to each bill-level prediction.

The preparation separates outcome labels from model inputs and excludes the current bill from selected historical windows. Grouping rules keep each record’s history aligned with its entity. Point-in-time validation checks which source values would be available when a reviewer requests a prediction.

## Compare candidates on a common evaluation path

## Evaluate ranking, probabilities, and time

**Grouped cross-validation.** The configured comparison uses ten folds grouped by BBO, the attorney identifier. Separate row- and NAC/case-grouped fold options are implemented. Holding an attorney’s related bills in one fold tests a different generalization question from splitting individual rows. It helps examine whether apparent performance depends on related records appearing on both sides of a split. Chronological tests add a complementary view of performance on later data.

**Several measures, different questions.** ROC AUC examines discrimination across thresholds. Brier score and log loss assess probability quality. Precision at a fixed review capacity, including P@50, measures the share of historically positive labels among the top-ranked bills at that capacity. That capacity-aware measure connects evaluation to the number of bills a human reviewer can assess.

**Calibration and backtesting.** I implemented Platt calibration using Spark logistic regression on the raw model probability, with a separate calibration window, a later test window, a buffer for delayed labels, and rolling time-based backtests. The separate windows distinguish model fitting, probability calibration, and assessment on later data. Calibration can change probability interpretation without necessarily improving ranking.

**Drift diagnostics.** Prediction summaries and feature-distribution comparisons help investigate changes over time. These diagnostics guide investigation of changing data and model behavior.

## Scoring outputs and next steps

The workflows implement evaluation-record and calibration registries, bill-scoring outputs, and ranked candidate views. Attorney and NAC/case history supply context for bill-level predictions. An amount-weighted ranking option multiplies a score by the bill amount to help prioritize review.
